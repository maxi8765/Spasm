Given names predict personality, partnership and progeny: a nine-type model derived from 5,318 names

David Hocking1 & Nicola J. L. Maxwell2

1The Clint Institute for Applied Name Theory, Queensland, Australia. 2Intense and Solemn Library Annex, Sydney, Australia.

A given name is the first social label most of us receive, and it is repeated to us, and about us, for the rest of our lives. Evidence that names carry affective value2, shape teachers' expectations3,4 and are eventually written on our faces5,6 suggests that the label may also shape the person. Here we describe the Name Theory™, in which each of 5,318 given names (3,012 female, 2,306 male) is scored on ten traits and assigned to one of nine Enneagram personality types and one of five sexual adventurer types. From these two assignments alone we derive the couple compatibility of any two people (81 type pairings), their sexual compatibility (25 pairings), the ideal personality type of their children, and the people they should kiss, marry or avoid. The model has been deployed to the public as the SPASM application. We find that the names of the present authors are 50% compatible as a couple and 80% compatible sexually, which we regard as appropriate for co-authors.

In the fourth edition of Why Shouldn't I Call My Son Clint?1, one of us (D.H.) set out short personality descriptions for several thousand common Australian names. The premise, in the book's words, is that each name evokes "some small and subconscious response from those we meet", and that over a lifetime this collective reaction "can gently trickle down and influence who we become"1. The descriptions were written from decades of fieldwork, which is to say meeting people.

The idea is less eccentric than it sounds. Names have measurable affective value2. Children whose names are viewed favourably by their teachers are expected to do better, and do3,4. People can match unfamiliar adult faces to their correct names at rates well above chance5, and personality traits are read from faces with some consistency across cultures6. Most strikingly, the face-name match holds for adults but not for children: people cannot match children's faces to their names, and machine-learning representations of adults who share a name are more similar to each other than those of adults with different names, while no such similarity appears among children7. A face, it seems, grows into its name. We see no reason a personality should not do the same.

The literature is not without its cautionary tales. Archival studies reported that people named Dennis or Denise are over-represented among dentists, and that people gravitate to cities resembling their names8. Re-analysis of the same data found little support for the effect9, a rejoinder disagreed10, and later work attributed much of it to confounds such as the birth cohorts of both names and professions11. We take two lessons. First, name effects must be modelled with care. Second, the SPASM database lists Dennis as "more like a rogue daffodil" than a dentist, which we consider consistent with the null result.

A nine-type model

Each name in the database was scored on ten traits: charisma, spontaneity, emotional savvy, sexual adventurousness, motivation, creativity, humour, spirituality, leadership and generosity (Methods). Charisma is scored on a scale of 1 to 10 and the remaining traits from 1 to 5, reflecting the authors' view that charisma matters about twice as much as everything else. The ten scores are summed to give the Hocko score, which ranged from 6 to 51 (female mean 32.4, s.d. 6.6; male mean 31.2, s.d. 5.9). The Hocko score is mapped onto the nine Enneagram types12 by fixed bands, with the highest scores (41 and above) assigned to type 7 and the lowest (24 and below) to type 5.

The resulting type distribution is shown in Fig. 1. Female names are spread almost evenly across the nine types. Male names are not: type 1 is the most common male type (349 names) and type 7 the rarest (144 names). We are unable to say whether this reflects something deep about men or something shallow about the authors, and leave the question to future work.

Couples

For any two people, the couple compatibility score is read from a 9 × 9 table of type pairings, each pairing carrying a score and five written descriptions, one of which is shown at random so that couples who consult the model repeatedly do not grow bored of each other. A second, independent sexual compatibility score is read from a 5 × 5 table of sexual adventurer types, each pairing with six descriptions. In the descriptions, the numerals for each type are replaced by the names of the two people, so that the reader learns, for example, that one partner is "better at the hands-on details of dealing with kids in canoes".

Across the 81 type pairings, couple compatibility ranges from 10% to 100% with a mean of 60% (Table 1). Eleven pairings score 100%, and nine score 10%. Sexual compatibility depends on the distance between two people's adventurer types: identical types score 100%, and the scores fall in steps of 20% as the types move apart, so that the most and least adventurous names score 20% together. The authors note that this is the only part of the model with an obvious physical analogue.

Progeny

The ideal personality type of a child is read from a 9 × 9 table indexed by the types of the two parents. Names of the chosen sex are then drawn at random from that type, five from common names and two from unusual ones, so that parents are offered both a safe option and a talking point. Applied to the present authors, the model recommends a type 4 child, a result we report here without further comment.

Kiss, marry or avoid

Each Enneagram type has a designated best and worst partner type. Candidates to marry are drawn from the best partner type and kept only if their sexual compatibility with the person exceeds 61%. Candidates to avoid are drawn from the worst partner type and kept only if their sexual compatibility is 60% or less, on the principle that a person should not be advised to avoid someone they would in fact get on with in at least one respect. Candidates to kiss are drawn from names with the same sexual adventurer type as the person, without regard to personality, which our informal testing suggests is how kissing generally works.

Validation

Formal validation is ongoing. In the meantime we offer three observations. The database entry for N.J.L.M. reads:

Intense and solemn like an encyclopaedia in a library, Nicola means well but she has twisted ideas about men and about the world. With lots of unconditional love, however, she'll be fine.

N.J.L.M. confirms that she means well. The entry for D.H. notes that "the heavy D's at both ends weigh his name down like the legs of a North Sea oil platform", which colleagues describe as broadly accurate. Finally, the couple compatibility of N.J.L.M. with her husband, whose name is described as the sound of a teaspoon being dropped onto a tiled floor, is 100%. We regard these three results as encouraging, and the sample size as a limitation.

We conclude that given names carry enough social weight to make a nine-type model of personality, partnership and progeny at least as entertaining as the alternatives. Readers are invited to test the model on themselves, their partners, their colleagues and their upcoming dates, and to submit any names we have missed.

Methods

Names were selected for being reasonably likely to be recognised by at least some English speakers, including nicknames that have replaced given names. Each name was scored by D.H. on the ten traits listed above and categorised as common (a; 3,157 names) or unusual (z; 2,161 names). Hocko scores were mapped to Enneagram types by the bands 41–60 (type 7), 38–40 (8), 36–37 (3), 34–35 (2), 32–33 (6), 30–31 (9), 28–29 (4), 25–27 (1) and 1–24 (5). Sexual adventurer type was taken directly from the sexual adventurousness score (1–5). Couple descriptions (405) and sexual compatibility descriptions (150) were written by D.H. All selection steps that draw names at random use a uniform shuffle, and results lists contain seven names where enough qualifying names exist. The full model is implemented in the SPASM application, and the source code is available from the authors' repository.
